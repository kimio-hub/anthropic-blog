Every answer needs a path back to its source

Summary

  • A reading assistant saves time only when checking its answer is cheaper than reading the source yourself. We call that property verifiability, and it depends mostly on where the evidence sits.
  • We compared four answer formats on the same 120 questions about 30 papers. Linking each claim to the passage that supports it, and quoting that passage, raised the share of claims a reviewer could verify within a minute from 18% to 86%.
  • Document-level links barely helped: they told reviewers which paper to open, not where to look. The gain came from precision, not from volume.
  • Claim-level citations expose a new failure — a real passage that does not support the claim — so the system needs a support check of its own before an answer is shown.

Why answers drift from their sources

Suppose you ask an assistant whether a paper’s method still works on small datasets. It replies that performance “drops sharply below 1,000 examples.” The sentence is plausible, specific and quick to read. It is also expensive to trust: to confirm it you need to find the experiment, the table and the condition it came from.

If the answer does not tell you where to look, you are back to skimming the paper, and the assistant has added a step rather than removed one. The problem is rarely that the model invents a claim from nothing. More often the claim is a paraphrase that has drifted: a trend reported for one dataset becomes a general rule, or a hedge in the original disappears.

Drift is hardest to catch when the answer is mostly right. Readers stop checking after the first few claims hold up, which is exactly when a stretched claim gets through.

So we framed the design question narrowly: for each sentence in an answer, how quickly can a reader find the evidence and decide whether it supports the sentence?1

What we measured

What we measured. We wrote 120 questions about 30 machine-learning papers and generated answers in four formats: no citations, a link to the document, a link to the supporting passage, and a link plus a short quotation of that passage. Twelve reviewers checked each claim with a one-minute limit and recorded whether they could confirm or refute it.

What we found. Passage-level evidence changed the task from searching to comparing. Reviewers verified most claims in the quoted format without opening the paper at all.

The jump between document links and passage links is the largest in the chart, and it is the cheapest to build: most retrieval systems already know which chunk they used. Quotations add a smaller gain, mainly on claims that paraphrase numbers.

A pattern for verifiable answers

The results suggest a simple pipeline. The important step is the one most systems skip: checking that each cited passage actually supports its claim before the answer reaches the reader.

Cite claims, not documents

A citation should resolve to the smallest unit a reader can check in one glance: a sentence, a table row, a figure panel. Answers that cite a whole document ask the reader to repeat the retrieval the system has already done.

Quote the passage, then paraphrase

When the claim is a number or a condition, show the original words next to it. Paraphrase is where drift enters; a quotation lets the reader see the gap between the two in a single look.

Check support before showing the answer

Claim-level citations make a new error possible: the passage exists and is on topic, but it does not say what the claim says. A second pass that asks “does this passage entail this sentence?” catches most of these before a reader does.

Implementation notes

We store each claim with the identifier of its passage and the exact span it quotes. Keeping the span, not only the page, is what lets the interface highlight evidence without another search.

answer.json{
  "claim": "Accuracy drops below 1,000 training examples.",
  "source": { "doc": "smith-2025", "section": "4.2", "span": [1832, 1967] },
  "quote": "…falls from 91% to 74% when fewer than 1,000 examples are used…",
  "support": "entailed"   // entailed | partial | unsupported
}

The format also changes what can be measured. Once every claim has a span, citation precision becomes a number you can track per release rather than an impression from spot checks.

FormatWhat the reader must doBuild costVerified in 1 min
No citationsFind the paper, then the passage, then compareNone18%
Document linkSearch inside the documentLow41%
Passage linkRead one passage and compareLow — retrieval already knows it73%
Link + quotationCompare two sentences side by sideMedium — needs span tracking86%

Limitations

  • Our questions were written by people who knew the papers. Real readers ask vaguer questions, which produce longer answers with more claims to check.
  • A one-minute limit rewards short evidence. Some claims legitimately need a figure and its caption, and our format quotes text only.
  • Reviewers were experienced readers of the field. Verifiability for newcomers may depend more on explanation than on citation.

What would change our conclusion

If support checking proved unreliable on long, multi-step claims, passage citations could create false confidence. We would want to see precision measured on such claims before recommending the format for high-stakes use.
